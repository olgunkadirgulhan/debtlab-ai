Managing multiple balances across different accounts can feel like a heavy burden. When a household faces a total debt of $16,000, finding a clear path forward is often the first step toward regaining financial clarity. The debt snowball method is a popular strategy that focuses on psychological wins by paying off the smallest balances first, regardless of the interest rates involved. This approach could provide the momentum needed to stay committed to a long-term repayment plan. By looking at a specific example featuring four distinct debts, a borrower might better understand how this strategy operates in a real-world context.

In this scenario, a borrower has a monthly budget of $840 to put toward their total debt of $16,000. This budget must cover the minimum payments on all accounts, with any remaining funds directed toward a single target debt. The objective is to eliminate one balance at a time, which may help simplify monthly chores and provide a sense of progress. Below is the breakdown of the current financial situation using both the snowball and avalanche methods for comparison.

|---|---|

| Snowball interest | $4,548 |
| Snowball first paid off | the store card |
| Avalanche time | 2 years |
| Avalanche interest | $3,684 |

## The Debt Snowball Payoff Order

To begin a debt snowball, a person first lists all their debts from the smallest balance to the largest balance. In this specific case, the four debts are Card A, Card B, a personal loan, and a store card. When we look at the balances, the order for a debt snowball would be as follows:

1. Store card: $900 at 26.99%
2. Card A: $2,500 at 24.99%
3. Personal loan: $5,200 at 11.5%
4. Card B: $7,400 at 29.99%

The store card, with its $900 balance, is the smallest. Even though Card B has a higher interest rate of 29.99%, the snowball method prioritizes the $900 balance first. The logic here is that seeing a debt disappear entirely may motivate the borrower to keep going. By focusing the $840 monthly budget on the store card while paying minimums on the others, the store card is the snowball first paid off. 

Once the $900 balance is gone, the borrower then takes the entire amount previously paid toward the store card and adds it to the payment for Card A, which has a $2,500 balance. This creates a larger monthly payment for the second debt, making the payoff process move faster. After Card A is eliminated, the cumulative payment is moved to the personal loan ($5,200), and finally to Card B ($7,400). 

## Time and Interest Costs for the Snowball Method

When following this specific snowball plan, the snowball time is estimated at 2 years and 1 month. During this period, the borrower might pay a total of $4,548 in interest. This interest cost occurs because the largest debt, Card B at $7,400, also carries the highest interest rate of 29.99%. Since the snowball method leaves this high-interest debt for last, interest continues to accrue on that large balance for a longer duration. 

While the snowball method could provide an earlier sense of accomplishment by clearing the store card quickly, it is important to understand the trade-offs. The $4,548 in interest is the price paid for the psychological benefits of the strategy. A borrower might find that the 2 years and 1 month timeframe is manageable, but they should also be aware of how other methods might compare.

## Comparing the Avalanche Method

For those who prefer a strictly mathematical approach, the avalanche method is an alternative. Instead of focusing on the balance size, the avalanche method prioritizes debts with the highest interest rates. In this scenario, the order of payoff would change significantly:

1. Card B: $7,400 at 29.99%
2. Store card: $900 at 26.99%
3. Card A: $2,500 at 24.99%
4. Personal loan: $5,200 at 11.5%

Because Card B has the highest rate at 29.99%, it is the avalanche first paid off. By targeting the most expensive debt first, the borrower could reduce the total interest paid over the life of the plan. According to the calculated scenario, the avalanche time is 2 years. This is one month shorter than the snowball method. Furthermore, the avalanche interest is $3,684. 

When we compare the two, the interest difference is $864. This means the cheaper method is avalanche. By choosing to pay off the 29.99% debt first, the borrower could save $864 and finish their journey one month earlier. ## Credit Score Considerations

Managing $16,000 in debt also has implications for a person's credit profile. Credit scores generally fall within a score range of 300 to 850. Within this range, lenders often look at specific score tiers to determine creditworthiness. These tiers include Poor 300-579, Fair 580-669, Good 670-739, Very good 740-799, and Exceptional 800-850. 

One of the most important aspects of credit health is the Fico weights assigned to different behaviors. Payment history is the most significant factor, accounting for 35% of a score. This is why making at least the minimum payments on all four debts is vital, regardless of which payoff method is chosen. Late payments can stay on credit reports up to 7 years, which may negatively impact a person's ability to secure favorable rates in the future. 

Another major factor is amounts owed, which makes up 30% of the Fico score calculation. This includes the utilization guideline, which suggests keeping credit usage under 30%, and ideally under 10% is often better. With a total debt of $16,000, a borrower's utilization might be high. As the snowball or avalanche plan progresses and the balances on the $2,500 Card A, $7,400 Card B, and $900 store card decrease, the utilization ratio could improve, which may positively influence the credit score. 

Other factors in the Fico weights include length of history at 15%, new credit at 10%, and credit mix at 10%. While the payoff strategy primarily affects the "amounts owed" category, it is helpful to remember that hard inquiries can stay on reports for 2 years and usually matter most in the first 12 months. When a person is focused on paying off $16,000 in debt, they might choose to avoid new hard inquiries to help stabilize their score.

## Deciding Which Path to Take

Choosing between the snowball and avalanche methods often comes down to personal preference and financial goals. The snowball method, with its total interest of $4,548 and a timeline of 2 years and 1 month, offers the psychological boost of paying off the store card first. For some, this win is worth more than the $864 they might save with the alternative method.

On the other hand, the avalanche method is the more cost-effective choice in this example. Saving $864 in interest and finishing the plan in exactly 2 years might be the primary motivation for a different borrower. The $864 saved could then be used for other financial goals, such as building an emergency fund or contributing to a savings account. 

Regardless of the chosen method, the key is consistency. Using a $840 monthly budget effectively requires discipline. Whether a borrower targets the $900 store card first or the 29.99% interest rate on Card B, the act of following a structured plan is a significant step forward. Tracking progress monthly and seeing the total $16,000 balance decline can provide its own form of motivation. By understanding the math and the credit implications, a person may feel more empowered to take control of their financial future.
