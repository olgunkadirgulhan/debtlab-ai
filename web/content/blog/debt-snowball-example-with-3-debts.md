Managing multiple balance obligations could feel challenging, but structured repayment strategies provide a clear, organized path forward. In this guide, we examine a debt snowball example with 3 debts to illustrate how focusing on smaller balances could help you build early momentum. Using exact numbers from our scenario calculations, we explain how prioritizing smaller obligations may simplify your monthly budget while steadily reducing total debt over time.

## Scenario Overview: Three Debts and One Monthly Budget

To understand how the debt snowball methodology operates in practice, consider a personal financial profile involving three separate balance accounts that add up to a total debt of $17,500. A dedicated monthly budget of $890 is allocated to clear these balances.

Here is the exact breakdown of the three balances included in this scenario:

- Store card: $1,200 at 18.99%
- Visa: $6,800 at 27.49%
- Car loan: $9,500 at 7.9%

| Avalanche time | 1 year and 11 months |

With a total debt of $17,500 and a committed monthly budget of $890, the debt snowball strategy organizes the repayment process by focusing entirely on balance size rather than interest rates.

## How the Debt Snowball Strategy Works

The debt snowball strategy prioritizes balance payoff order from the smallest balance to the largest balance. Regardless of interest rates, extra payment effort is directed toward the account with the lowest dollar amount owed.

In our scenario, the balance amounts are structured as follows:

- The store card carries a balance of $1,200 at 18.99%.
- The Visa carries a balance of $6,800 at 27.49%.
- The car loan carries a balance of $9,500 at 7.9%.

Under the debt snowball method, the store card is targeted first because its balance of $1,200 is smaller than the $6,800 Visa balance and the $9,500 car loan balance. Required minimum payments are maintained on the Visa and car loan to keep those accounts current, while all remaining funds from the monthly budget of $890 are applied toward the store card.

Once the store card balance of $1,200 at 18.99% is fully paid off, the store card is eliminated. The entire payment amount previously used for the store card rolls into the repayment amount for the next smallest balance, which is the Visa at $6,800 at 27.49%. Finally, after the Visa balance is eliminated, the full monthly budget of $890 is concentrated on the remaining car loan balance of $9,500 at 7.9%.

This sequential rolling of payments creates a psychological snowball effect. Paying off the first balance quickly could boost motivation and provide tangible proof of progress early in the payoff journey.

## Detailed Walkthrough of the Debt Snowball Timeline

Let us trace how the repayment process unfolds across the total debt of $17,500 using the monthly budget of $890.

First, the strategy focuses on the store card balance of $1,200 at 18.99%. Because this account has the lowest balance, the snowball first paid off option is the store card. Eliminating this balance early removes one monthly obligation from your financial list.

Second, with the store card paid off, the freed-up funds join the regular payments for the Visa balance of $6,800 at 27.49%. Although the Visa carries a higher interest rate than the store card, the debt snowball places it second because its balance of $6,800 is larger than the store card balance of $1,200 but smaller than the car loan balance of $9,500.

Third, once the Visa balance of $6,800 at 27.49% is cleared, the combined payment capacity focuses on the car loan balance of $9,500 at 7.9%.

Under this framework:
- Total snowball time required to reach complete payoff is 1 year and 11 months.
- Total snowball interest paid across all accounts equals $2,579.

By maintaining the monthly budget of $890 consistently, the entire total debt of $17,500 is eliminated in 1 year and 11 months.

## Comparing Snowball and Avalanche Methods

When evaluating debt payoff plans, comparing the debt snowball with the debt avalanche method provides helpful perspective. While the snowball method orders debts by balance size (smallest to largest), the avalanche method orders debts by interest rate (highest to lowest).

Here is how the outcomes compare for our total debt of $17,500 with a monthly budget of $890:

Under the debt snowball method:
- First paid off: the store card
- Total payoff timeline: 1 year and 11 months
- Total interest cost: $2,579

Under the debt avalanche method:
- First paid off: the Visa
- Total payoff timeline: 1 year and 11 months
- Total interest cost: $2,454

Because the debt avalanche method targets the Visa balance of $6,800 first due to its high interest rate of 27.49%, it reduces interest accumulation faster. In this comparison:
- The cheaper method is the avalanche strategy.
- The interest difference between the two approaches is $125.

Notice that both methods achieve complete repayment within the exact same overall timeframe of 1 year and 11 months. However, choosing avalanche saves $125 in interest because the Visa carries a higher rate than the store card at 18.99% and the car loan at 7.9%.

## Credit Score Impact and Key Considerations

Executing a repayment plan using a monthly budget of $890 directly influences several factors that shape your credit profile. Understanding Fico weights helps clarify how debt payoff intersects with credit scoring:

- Payment history: 35% of your score weight.
- Amounts owed: 30% of your score weight.
- Length of history: 15% of your score weight.
- New credit: 10% of your score weight.
- Credit mix: 10% of your score weight.

Standard FICO scoring scales range from a score range of 300 to 850. Score tiers are defined as follows:
- Poor: 300-579
- Fair: 580-669
- Good: 670-739
- Very good: 740-799
- Exceptional: 800-850

Consistently dedicating $890 per month to maintain prompt payments reinforces payment history (35%), which is the single largest component of credit scores. Maintaining a clean payment history is essential because late payments can stay on credit reports up to 7 years.

Furthermore, reducing your total debt of $17,500 improves amounts owed (30%), commonly measured via credit utilization. General guidelines suggest keeping balance utilization under 30%, and under 10% is often better for credit health. Payoff strategies that rapidly lower revolving credit lines, such as paying off the store card at $1,200 or the Visa at $6,800, could help lower your overall utilization ratio faster.

If you consider applying for new credit accounts or refinancing during your repayment journey, note that hard inquiries can stay on reports for 2 years and usually matter most in the first 12 months. Since there are 12 months per year, staying committed to your monthly budget of $890 over the full timeline of 1 year and 11 months allows your credit profile to strengthen as debts decrease and credit account age grows.

## Choosing the Right Approach for Your Financial Goals

Selecting between the debt snowball and debt avalanche methods ultimately depends on whether you value immediate psychological wins or maximum interest savings.

The debt snowball method provides quick feedback. By eliminating the store card balance of $1,200 at 18.99% early, you reduce your total number of open balances from three to two in short order. This quick victory could build confidence and help you maintain momentum over the entire 1 year and 11 months payoff duration.

On the other hand, the debt avalanche method focuses on mathematical efficiency. By eliminating the Visa balance of $6,800 at 27.49% first, the avalanche method lowers total interest paid to $2,454 compared to $2,579 under snowball. This creates an interest difference of $125 in savings, making avalanche the cheaper method.

Both methods successfully wipe out the total debt of $17,500 within 1 year and 11 months using the monthly budget of $890. Evaluating your personal discipline and financial priorities could help you select the strategy that best fits your needs.
