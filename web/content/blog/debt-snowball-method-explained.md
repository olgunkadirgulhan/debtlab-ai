Managing multiple balances across various accounts can feel overwhelming, especially when interest rates vary significantly. The debt snowball method is a popular strategy designed to help individuals regain control of their finances by focusing on psychological wins. Rather than looking strictly at interest rates, this approach prioritizes paying off the smallest balances first. This could provide the momentum needed to stay committed to a long-term repayment plan. By seeing accounts closed one by one, a person may find it easier to stick to their monthly budget and eventually reach a state of being debt-free.

At DebtLab, we believe in looking at the numbers honestly. While the debt snowball is effective for motivation, it is not always the most cost-efficient path. Understanding the trade-offs between different methods is essential for making an informed decision. Below, we have outlined a specific scenario involving a total debt of $16,000 to show how the math works in practice.

## How the Debt Snowball Method Works

The debt snowball method is a behavioral strategy. The core idea is to list all of your debts from the smallest balance to the largest balance. You ignore the interest rates for the purpose of ordering your payments. Every month, you pay the minimum amount due on every debt except for the smallest one. Any extra funds in your monthly budget are directed toward that smallest balance until it is entirely gone.

Once the smallest debt is paid off, you take the entire amount you were paying toward it—both the minimum payment and the extra cash—and roll it into the next smallest debt. This process continues, with the payment "snowballing" as each debt is eliminated. By the time you reach your largest balance, your monthly payment toward that single account has grown significantly because it includes the combined minimums of all the previous debts you have already settled.

In our scenario, the smallest debt is the store card with a balance of $900 at 26.99%. Under the snowball method, this would be the first priority. Even though Card B has a higher interest rate of 29.99%, the snowball method focuses on the $900 balance first to clear the account quickly. This approach is designed to provide an early sense of achievement.

## Step-by-Step Math: The $16,000 Example

To see how this works, let’s look at a total debt of $16,000. In this example, the individual has a monthly budget of $540 to put toward their balances. The debts consist of Card A at $2,500 with a 24.99% rate, Card B at $7,400 with a 29.99% rate, a Personal loan of $5,200 at 11.5%, and a store card of $900 at 26.99%.

### Step 1: List and Rank
First, the debts are ranked by balance size:
1. Store card: $900
2. Card A: $2,500
3. Personal loan: $5,200
4. Card B: $7,400

### Step 2: Allocate the Budget
With a monthly budget of $540, the individual pays the minimums on Card A, Card B, and the Personal loan. Every remaining cent is applied to the $900 store card. Because $900 is the smallest figure in the list, it becomes the first target. According to our computations, the snowball first paid off the store card. 

### Step 3: Rolling the Payments
After the store card is gone, the money previously used for it is moved to Card A, which has a balance of $2,500. This increases the speed at which the $2,500 balance decreases. Once Card A is finished, the combined payments move to the Personal loan of $5,200, and finally to the largest balance, the $7,400 on Card B.

Using this specific strategy, the total time to reach a zero balance for the $16,000 total would be 3 years and 11 months. Over this period, the snowball interest paid would total $9,012.

## Comparing the Snowball to the Avalanche

While the snowball method focuses on balance size, the debt avalanche method prioritizes interest rates. In an avalanche, you pay off the debt with the highest interest rate first. This is mathematically the cheapest way to get out of debt, but it might take longer to see the first account disappear if the high-interest balance is large.

In our scenario, the avalanche method would target the debts differently. Our calculator shows that the avalanche time would be 3 years and 9 months, which is 2 months faster than the snowball. Additionally, the avalanche interest is $8,260. When you compare this to the $9,012 interest from the snowball method, the interest difference is $752. This means the cheaper method is avalanche for this specific set of numbers.

Interestingly, in this computed scenario, the avalanche first paid off the personal loan. While the personal loan has the lowest interest rate at 11.5%, the overall mathematical structure of these specific balances and the $540 monthly budget resulted in this payoff order. ## The Impact on Your Credit Score

When you are paying down $16,000 in debt, your credit score may change over time. Credit scores generally fall within a score range of 300 to 850. Lenders use these numbers to determine your creditworthiness, often grouping scores into tiers. These tiers include Poor 300-579, Fair 580-669, Good 670-739, Very good 740-799, and Exceptional 800-850.

Several factors influence where you fall in these tiers, and the debt snowball method interacts with these factors in specific ways:

### Amounts Owed and Utilization
Amounts owed accounts for 30% of the FICO weights. A major part of this is your credit utilization ratio, which is the amount of credit you are using compared to your limits. A general utilization guideline is to keep your usage under 30%, and staying under 10% is often better. As you pay off the $2,500 on Card A or the $7,400 on Card B, your total utilization drops, which could positively affect your score.

### Payment History
The most significant factor is payment history, which makes up 35% of the score. Whether you use the snowball or avalanche method, making consistent payments is vital. It is important to remember that late payments can stay on credit reports up to 7 years. By committing to a monthly budget of $540 and ensuring all minimums are met, you protect this crucial part of your credit profile.

### Length of History and New Credit
Other factors include length of history at 15%, new credit at 10%, and credit mix at 10%. If you apply for new loans to consolidate your $16,000 debt, be aware of hard inquiries. These hard inquiries can stay on reports for 2 years and usually matter most in the first 12 months. Keeping your older accounts open, even after the $900 store card is paid off, may help maintain your length of history.

## Practical Steps to Start Your Snowball

If you decide that the psychological motivation of the snowball method is right for you, here are the steps you could take to begin:

1. **Identify all balances:** Gather your statements for all accounts. In our example, this included the $2,500, $7,400, $5,200, and $900 balances.
2. **Verify interest rates:** Note the rates, such as the 29.99% on Card B or the 11.5% on the personal loan, so you understand the cost of your debt.
3. **Calculate your budget:** Determine exactly how much you can pay each month. If your monthly budget is $540, you must ensure this covers all minimum payments with some left over for the snowball.
4. **Order by balance:** Ignore the interest rates and list the debts from smallest to largest.
5. **Automate minimums:** To avoid the risk of late payments that could stay on your report for 7 years, set up automatic minimum payments for every account.
6. **Focus extra cash:** Direct the remainder of your $540 budget to the smallest balance.

## Is the Snowball Method Right for You?

Choosing between the snowball and avalanche methods often comes down to a choice between speed of motivation and speed of savings. The snowball method for this $16,000 debt takes 3 years and 11 months. If you are the type of person who needs to see progress quickly to stay on track, paying off the store card first might be the key to your success.

However, you must be comfortable with the fact that you may pay more in interest. In this case, choosing the snowball over the avalanche could cost an extra $752 in interest over the life of the debt. For some, $752 is a fair price to pay for the psychological benefit of closing accounts sooner. For others, saving that $752 by using the avalanche method—which would take 3 years and 9 months—is the priority.

Every financial situation is unique. There are 12 months per year to manage your payments, and consistency is the most important factor in any plan. Whether your score is currently in the Poor 300-579 tier or the Good 670-739 tier, reducing your total debt is a positive step toward financial stability. By understanding the math of the $9,012 in snowball interest versus the $8,260 in avalanche interest, you can choose the path that best fits your personal needs and goals.
